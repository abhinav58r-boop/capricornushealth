Fluid shifts

Without gravity pulling fluid toward your feet, roughly 1 to 2 L moves toward your head and chest. Expect a puffy face, stuffy nose and pressure behind the eyes. Over weeks, pressure changes can affect vision (SANS). Your heart also works less and loses some conditioning, so standing up after landing can cause fainting. Counter with fluids and salt as prescribed, daily aerobic exercise and lower-body negative pressure sessions.

Bone density loss

Weight-bearing bones in the hips, spine and legs can lose about 1 to 1.5% of density per month in orbit, much faster than osteoporosis on Earth. Bones need load to stay strong. Resistive exercise (squats, deadlifts, heel raises), enough calcium and vitamin D, and keeping to your exercise minutes are your best defense.

Muscle and strength

Calves, thighs and back muscles are not needed to hold you up, so they shrink quickly. Skipped workouts add up: three light days in a row is a trend worth correcting. Mix resistance and aerobic training and eat enough protein.

Radiation

Beyond Earth's atmosphere you are exposed to galactic cosmic rays and occasional solar particle events, at doses many times Earth's background. Long-term risks include cancer, cardiovascular disease and effects on the nervous system. Check your dosimeter, move to the shielded area when a solar storm alert is issued, and report unusual fatigue or nausea.

Sleep and behavioral health

Orbiting Earth gives you about 16 sunrises a day, which disrupts your body clock. Isolation, confinement and workload make it harder. Keep a fixed sleep window, use dim warm light before bed, stay in touch with family and crew, and ask for a private psychological conference when mood stays low. Asking for help early is part of mission readiness.
